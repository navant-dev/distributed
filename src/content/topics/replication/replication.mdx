<QuickRefresher>Replication keeps multiple copies of data for availability, read scale, durability, and geographic proximity. Common shapes are leader/follower, multi-leader, and leaderless; each moves ordering and conflict work to a different place.</QuickRefresher>

## How it works

In leader/follower replication, one node orders writes and streams them to followers. Synchronous acknowledgement waits for selected followers. Asynchronous acknowledgement responds earlier and lets replicas catch up later.

<KeyInsight title="A replica is not automatically a backup">Replicas quickly copy both valid changes and accidental deletes. Recovery still needs point-in-time backups, tested restore procedures, and protection from correlated failure.</KeyInsight>

<TradeoffTable rows={[
  {choice:"Synchronous",gain:"Lower acknowledged-data loss and fresher followers",cost:"Write latency and sensitivity to slow replicas"},
  {choice:"Asynchronous",gain:"Fast writes and fault isolation",cost:"Lag and possible loss after leader failure"},
  {choice:"Multi-leader",gain:"Local writes in multiple regions",cost:"Conflict detection and resolution"}
]} />

<FailureScenario title="Leader fails after local write, before replication">If the client received success too early, the promoted follower may not contain the write. The system has acknowledged durability it cannot preserve.</FailureScenario>

<FailureScenario title="A follower falls hours behind">Serving reads from it expands user-visible staleness. Rejoining may require a snapshot rather than replaying an unbounded log, and catch-up traffic can overload the leader.</FailureScenario>

<InterviewQuestion level="Intermediate" question="What is the difference between replication and consensus?">Replication copies state. Consensus makes participants agree on which decisions are authoritative and in what order despite failures. A replication topology may use consensus, but the concepts are not interchangeable.</InterviewQuestion>
